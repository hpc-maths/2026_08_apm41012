# Essential notions

In this section, a [first notebook](./peano.ipynb) illustrates how important the well-posedness at the theoretical level is for the systems of differential equations we study, in order to make their numerical solution possible. To this end, we attempt the numerical integration of a differential equation whose nonlinearity satisfies the assumptions of Peano's theorem but does not have the Lipschitz property that guarantees uniqueness of the solution and continuous dependence on the data. We identify the difficulties that this can raise, making a numerical solution ineffective.

Then we experiment with the two explicit and implicit Euler schemes studied in the lecture on several of these systems. The aim here is to gain a good understanding of the notions of absolute stability and of accuracy, in connection with the notion of stiffness and its impact on the numerical solution. [The study of the Hirschfelder and Curtiss system](./curtiss.ipynb) is enlightening and makes it possible to understand why explicit methods struggle with stiff systems, whereas the implicit method, even though it is just as inaccurate since it is first order, has very good absolute stability properties: the implicit Euler scheme is even A-stable.

[A second application of the two numerical methods to conservative systems](./conservative_systems.ipynb) coming from celestial mechanics clearly illustrates the inadequacy of these schemes, designed for dissipative systems, when it comes to solving conservative systems. Indeed, these methods are not designed to preserve the invariant of the system, here the gravitational energy.

Finally, we focus on [the thermal explosion problem](./explosion.ipynb), which combines a strong stiffness with an explosive dynamics involving an eigenvalue with a positive and very large real part. This problem illustrates the difficulties encountered with each of the two methods, be it in terms of stability with respect to perturbations with the implicit method in the explosive zone, or absolute stability with the explicit method in the end-of-reaction zone, which involves an eigenvalue with a very large negative real part and therefore a very strong stiffness.
